AI Use Journal

Author: Roberto Pablo Cuevas Pinto

Tools and scope

I used OpenAI ChatGPT for mathematical explanations, JavaScript and SVG suggestions, debugging, and review of the English text. The exact model identifiers for the earlier exchanges were not recorded. I chose the scope of the project, applied changes on my GitHub branch, and tested the published interactions in the browser.

Significant exchanges

The prompts and responses below are summarized in English (originally the prompts were worked in spanish).

1. From worked examples to computed perimeter groups

Prompt: Can the program compute how to group the sides of a regular polygon from its number of sides, instead of storing a grouping for each example?

Response and use: I began with constructions for specific polygons to understand how the pieces fit and how their rotations move the original boundary inward. After trying fixed groups, I raised the idea of extending the project with a computed construction. ChatGPT helped turn that idea into an algorithm: calculate the maximum group size, distribute the groups around the perimeter, and construct each piece from its chain of sides and its rotated copy.

Decision and check: I replaced the fixed groupings with this calculation and added a number-of-sides input. I checked examples in the browser, including a polygon with 60 sides. I set 60 as the interface limit because adding more sides made little visible difference at the scale of the drawing. This is a display choice, not a mathematical limit.

2. Letting the visitor draw a simple polygon

Prompt: Could the visitor draw a polygon and see how the construction with at most 2n + 1 pieces behaves on their own input?

Response and use: ChatGPT helped separate the interaction into drawing, closing and validating the polygon, and showing the cuts and reconstruction. I chose to give visitors control over the input so they could explore which outlines the demo accepts and follow the original boundary through the rearrangement.

Decision and check: I made closing the polygon a separate action so the visitor does not have to tap the first vertex again. I tested drawn polygons in the browser and checked that the editor showed a closing edge and gave feedback when a construction could not be displayed.

3. Choosing and checking the initial cut angle

Prompt: Can the initial angle of 16.26° be changed, and why start there?

Response and use: ChatGPT explained that this angle gives an initial cut depth of 14% of the side length. I kept it as a starting value and documented that the program halves the angle when a candidate fails its geometric checks.

Check: During development, a narrow rectangle was used to check that the program could reject the first candidate and try a smaller angle.

4. Explaining a rejected challenge attempt

Prompt: Do the pieces overlap when two long groups meet in the decagon?

Response and correction: The first explanation said that the pieces pinched the centre. On closer inspection, the two outer pieces share a cut, and the computed central outline traverses it in both directions. The rejection therefore concerns the outline generated by this implementation. It does not establish that the grouping is impossible.

Decision and check: I kept the simpler constructor and checked the visual feedback in the browser. The shared cut is highlighted in orange, while a group that exceeds the challenge's size rule is highlighted in red.

5. Designing the perimeter-grouping challenge

Prompt: How can visitors choose their own perimeter groups without having to draw arbitrary cuts? Which polygons make the challenge varied while keeping it easy to use?

Response and use: ChatGPT suggested letting visitors mark vertices where groups begin, then generating the cuts with the same constructor used by the automatic demo. I chose to limit the marks to vertices so visitors could focus on the size and order of the groups. The automatic demo still supports groups that end halfway along a side, as in the 7- and 9-sided examples.

Decision and check: I started with a fixed decagon, then added three more polygons. The octagon introduces the interaction with groups of two sides. The decagon shows how changing the order of the same group sizes can change the result. The 13-sided polygon requires five marks, and the 18-sided polygon gives visitors more room to experiment with group sizes. I checked that the challenge worked after adding the selector and that the automatic comparator still worked after the two demos began sharing a constructor.
