1. Introduction - Computational Geometry - ULB

Inside-Out Dissections

Author: Roberto Pablo Cuevas Pinto

Can we cut a polygon into pieces and put them back together so that the original boundary moves to the interior?

An inside-out dissection does this with rotations and translations. The pieces form a congruent copy of the original polygon, but the new outline is made from segments that were originally internal cuts.

This project explores two results from Akpanya, Rivkin and Stock: a construction with at most 2n + 1 pieces for a simple polygon with n sides, and a bound of six pieces for regular polygons. Read the paper (CCCG 2025).

Start with a triangle

Aaron Meyerowitz's construction cuts a triangle into two smaller triangles and two parallelograms. Follow the dashed segments from Cuts to Reconstruction.

Construction by Aaron Meyerowitz. The triangles exchange positions by translation, and each parallelogram makes a half-turn.

From a triangle to a polygon

A simple polygon with n vertices can be triangulated into n - 2 triangles without adding vertices. Rebuild each triangle inside out in its original position. Each original boundary edge lies on one of those triangles, so its marked segments move into the polygon's interior.

Can we guarantee fewer pieces without triangulating first?

2. The general construction

Two triangles per side

The edge-based construction improves the triangulation bound for five or more sides. For a pentagon, the two bounds are 11 and 12 pieces. Neither bound claims a minimum.

2n + 1 pieces n is the number of sides

How a pair of pieces works

  1. Construct the pair. For the directed edge A -> B, let M be its midpoint. With the polygon oriented counterclockwise, rotate the vector AM about A by +α to get C, and BM about B by -α to get D. The two triangles are congruent by side-angle-side.
  2. Exchange the pair. Map A to B with a rotation of π - α, and use the inverse motion for its partner. The first piece's marked segment AM maps to BD, which was an internal cut.
  3. Check the candidate. The editor checks that the input boundary is simple. For each candidate angle it checks containment of the triangles, overlap between triangles, and simplicity of the central boundary. It then checks that the moved pieces fit their intended positions.

The editor starts at about 16.26 degrees, why? The first trial uses α = arctan(7/24). For a side of length L, the cut tip lies (L/2) sin α inward from that side. Since sin α = 7/25, this starts with a cut depth of 14% of the side length. This makes the first cuts visible and gives a consistent starting point across scales. The editor halves the angle when a candidate fails the cut checks, up to 20 attempts. In a narrow rectangle, pieces on opposite sides can overlap even if the corners leave room. In a polygon with a notch, a cut can leave the polygon even when its tip is inside. The implementation uses numerical tolerances; exhausting the search does not establish impossibility.

3. Regular polygons

What does symmetry change?

Can symmetry reduce the number of pieces? Proposition 1 gives a six-piece upper bound for regular polygons. Start with the small cases, then compare both constructions on the same larger polygon.

Small regular polygons

Triangle, square and pentagon use separate constructions in Proposition 1. The four-piece construction above works for any triangle, including an equilateral one.

These three polygons all satisfy the six-piece bound, but the rule for grouping sides below is used for polygons with six or more sides

Grouping sides for n ≥ 6

Take a chain of consecutive sides and rotate it 180 degrees about the midpoint between its endpoints. The two chains enclose a piece with central symmetry. A half-turn exchanges the outer chain with the chain formed by internal cuts.

For a block of k full sides, the angle at either end of the piece is 2π (k - 1)/n. At a shared vertex, two neighbouring blocks must fit within the polygon's interior angle. Requiring each block to use at most half of that angle gives:

k = ⌊(n + 2)/4⌋
Piece bound: ⌈n/k⌉ + 1 ≤ 6

The extra piece accounts for the central region when one remains. This is an upper bound, not a minimum. For example, the hexagon needs no central piece in the three-rhombus construction. For the heptagon and nonagon, this demo splits one side at its midpoint to avoid a block made of a single straight segment.

Source: Akpanya, Rivkin and Stock, CCCG 2025, Proposition 1.
